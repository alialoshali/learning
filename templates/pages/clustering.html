Why it exists: we often have lots of data and no labels, and want to know which items naturally belong together: customer segments, topics in documents, species in gene data, colours in an image. Clustering splits data into groups so that items in a group are similar. Along the way it introduces two ideas every Transformer depends on: measuring similarity between vectors, and softly assigning a point to the things it is similar to, which is attention.

01Similarity: distance, dot product, cosine

“Similar” must become a number. For two vectors \(a,b\in\mathbb R^d\) there are three standard choices:

$$\text{distance: }\lVert a-b\rVert=\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_j(a_j-b_j)^2}$$$$\text{dot product: }a\cdot b=\textstyle\sum_j a_jb_j\qquad\text{cosine: }\cos\theta=\frac{a\cdot b}{\lVert a\rVert\,\lVert b\rVert}$$
  • Distance: small = similar. Depends on position and length.
  • Dot product: large = similar. Grows with the lengths of both vectors.
  • Cosine: only the angle. +1 = same direction, 0 = unrelated (perpendicular), −1 = opposite.

They are tied together by \(\lVert a-b\rVert^2=\lVert a\rVert^2+\lVert b\rVert^2-2\,a\cdot b\): for vectors of equal length, a small distance means a large dot product.

Try it: drag the tips of the two arrows on the right and watch the three numbers change. Make them point the same way but with different lengths: cosine stays at 1, distance does not.

Transformer linkAttention scores are dot products \(q\cdot k\) between a query and every key, divided by \(\sqrt{d}\) to keep their size under control. Retrieval for RAG ranks documents by cosine similarity of embeddings.

02k-means, step by step

k-means represents each of \(K\) clusters by its centre (the centroid \(\mu_k\)) and alternates two simple steps:

  1. Assign: each point joins its nearest centroid.
    $$c_i=\arg\min_{k}\ \lVert x_i-\mu_k\rVert^2$$
  2. Update: each centroid moves to the mean of its points.
    $$\mu_k=\frac{1}{|S_k|}\sum_{i\in S_k}x_i$$

Repeat until nothing changes. The coloured background shows which centroid “owns” each region (a Voronoi diagram). The boundaries are always straight lines halfway between two centroids.

Try it: press Step repeatedly and read the phase label. Then try Run, change \(K\), click to add points, and switch datasets. On “moons” and “rings” k-means fails, because it can only make round, convex groups.

03The objective, convergence and bad starts

k-means is minimising one number, the within-cluster sum of squares (inertia):

$$J(c,\mu)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lVert x_i-\mu_{c_i}\rVert^2$$
  • The assign step minimises \(J\) over \(c\) with \(\mu\) fixed.
  • The update step minimises \(J\) over \(\mu\) with \(c\) fixed (the mean is the point with the smallest total squared distance).

So \(J\) never goes up, and the algorithm always stops. This is coordinate descent. But it only finds a local minimum: a bad start can leave two centroids sharing one blob while another blob is split.

Try it: press 30 restarts to run k-means from 30 random starts on the 5-blob data. The histogram shows how often each final \(J\) occurs. k-means++ picks starting centroids far apart (each new one chosen with probability ∝ squared distance to the nearest existing one) and finds the best solution far more often.

Transformer linkTraining a neural network is also descent on a non-convex loss with many local minima. There we rely on a good random initialisation (Xavier/He) and on noise from mini-batches, the same lesson as k-means++.

04How many clusters? Elbow and silhouette

\(K\) is a hyper-parameter, and \(J\) cannot choose it: \(J\) always gets smaller as \(K\) grows, reaching 0 when every point is its own cluster. Two common tools:

  • Elbow method: plot \(J\) against \(K\). Choose the \(K\) where the curve bends and adding clusters stops helping much.
  • Silhouette score: for each point, compare \(a\) = mean distance to its own cluster with \(b\) = mean distance to the nearest other cluster:
    $$s_i=\frac{b_i-a_i}{\max(a_i,b_i)}\in[-1,1]$$
    The average \(s\) is high when clusters are tight and well separated. Pick the \(K\) with the highest score.

Change the dataset in section 02 and look at both curves here. On “no structure” there is no clear elbow and silhouettes are low. That is an honest answer: there are no real clusters.

Teaching tipClustering always returns some grouping, even for random noise. Always check whether the groups are real (silhouette, stability across restarts, domain sense).

05Beyond k-means: density-based clustering (DBSCAN)

k-means assumes round clusters of similar size. DBSCAN defines a cluster as a dense region of points, whatever its shape:

  • A point is a core point if at least minPts points lie within radius \(\varepsilon\).
  • Core points within \(\varepsilon\) of each other are joined into the same cluster, and their neighbours are added as border points.
  • Everything else is noise (grey). No \(K\) is needed.

Try it: on moons and rings, DBSCAN finds the true shapes where k-means fails. Make \(\varepsilon\) too small and everything becomes noise; too large and everything merges into one cluster.

06Soft assignment: k-means meets attention

k-means makes a hard choice: each point belongs 100% to one centroid. A soft version gives every point a weight for every centroid, using a softmax over negative squared distances with temperature \(T\):

$$r_{ik}=\frac{\exp\!\big(-\lVert x_i-\mu_k\rVert^2/T\big)}{\sum_{j}\exp\!\big(-\lVert x_i-\mu_j\rVert^2/T\big)}\qquad\mu_k=\frac{\sum_i r_{ik}\,x_i}{\sum_i r_{ik}}$$

As \(T\to0\) this becomes ordinary k-means. With larger \(T\), points near a boundary are shared. (A Gaussian mixture model trained with the EM algorithm is the probabilistic version of the same idea.)

Now compare it with attention:

$$\text{Attention}(q)=\sum_k\underbrace{\mathrm{softmax}_k\!\Big(\frac{q\cdot k_k}{\sqrt d}\Big)}_{\text{weights, sum to 1}}\;v_k$$

Same structure: a similarity between the query and each key, a softmax to turn similarities into weights, then a weighted average. Hover over any point to see its weight bars.

Transformer linkAn attention head performs a soft, learned “which group of tokens am I most similar to?” lookup at every layer. Some research even shows trained attention heads behaving like soft clustering of tokens. Lowering the temperature (or scaling up \(q\cdot k\)) makes attention “harder”, just as here.

07Application: colour quantisation (vector quantisation)

Cluster the pixels of an image by colour: each pixel is a 3-D vector (R, G, B). With \(K\) clusters, replace every pixel by its centroid colour. The image is now stored as a palette of K colours plus one small index per pixel, which is a big compression.

This is called vector quantisation: map a continuous vector to the nearest entry of a learned codebook.

Try it: lower \(K\). At \(K=2\) to \(4\) you get a poster-like image; around 16 colours most viewers can hardly tell the difference.

Transformer linkVector quantisation is how images and audio are turned into discrete tokens for Transformers: VQ-VAE / VQ-GAN codebooks map each image patch to one of a few thousand codebook vectors, and the Transformer then models those token ids like words. Vector databases for RAG use the same trick (product quantisation) to search billions of embeddings quickly.
